# Part 0 - The Spine: project, behavioural, site, safety and L&T-specific
**Every candidate gets these. This is where mechanical GET panels are actually decided.**

Mechanical GET panels do not fail candidates on hard derivations. They fail them on consistency: the
panel checks whether the story on your resume is the same story you tell when you are pushed. So the
master rule for this part is **the answer must be true, specific and short**. If a claim cannot be
defended in the follow-up, cut it from the first answer.

Each answer below is written to be *sayable in 30-60 seconds*. Read it, then say it out loud in your
own words. If it does not sound like you, it is not learned.

---

## 1. The opener - 90 seconds, and then STOP

### Q1. Tell me about yourself. [H]
**A:** Do NOT open with "Good morning, my name is..." - the panel has your resume. Go straight in:
academic background (one line), one concrete detail (one or two sentences), one skill with evidence
(one line), why you are here (one line). Then close the sentence and stop talking. Roughly 90 seconds.
Panels interrupt a rambling intro, and a long intro reads as nerves, not preparation.
**Push:** "Why not start with your name?" -> "I did not want to waste the first minute of your time;
you already have my resume in front of you, so I would rather start with what I have actually done."
**Trap:** a 2-minute autobiography. Also: "I am a hard worker and a team player" with no evidence.

### Q2. Why did you choose mechanical engineering? [H]
**A:** Because it is the broadest of the core engineering branches - it connects physics and maths to
something you can see built. Say the specific area that pulled you: machines, thermal, design, or
manufacturing. Then tie it to the role: you want to be in the part of engineering where a design
becomes a physical machine that has to work.
**Push:** "Why not electrical or civil?" -> Be honest and specific: "I am not saying those are
inferior. I chose mechanical because X, and I am aware that civil would have given me Y. What I want
to do is Z, and mechanical is where I can get to Z fastest."
**Trap:** "Because it is the core of every industry" - vague, and it invites the follow-up you cannot
answer.

### Q3. What is your favourite subject, and why? [H]
**A:** Pick the one you can actually defend for 10 minutes, not the one with the best marks. Give a
reason that shows understanding, not just liking: "I like X because it is where the abstract maths
finally turns into a physical outcome - you can see the machine behave the way the equation says it
will." Then be ready for the immediate follow-up: explain one concept from it.
**Push:** "Explain a concept from it." -> Have ONE ready, and be ready for a numerical.
**Trap:** naming Thermodynamics and then freezing when they ask for the Otto cycle. If thermo is your
favourite, the Otto and Diesel cycles are almost certain to be asked.

### Q4. Why L&T? [H]
**A:** "I want to work in heavy engineering, where the scale is large and the work reaches an actual
site. L&T is one of the few Indian companies that both designs the equipment and builds the plant it
goes into, so the drawing has to survive the shop floor and then survive the site. My internship at
BHEL Hyderabad showed me the manufacturing end of exactly that, and I want to be trained into that
chain."
Then name a **specific** L&T vertical or project you can talk about - power, heavy engineering,
machines and automation, or a construction project. If you do not know a specific one, say what you
know about the vertical you are targeting instead. Vague = fail.
**Push:** "Which business unit?" -> Name it and say why: "Because manufacturing technology and
project execution is where my interest in the process plan to inspection gate chain from the
internship would actually be used."
**Trap:** "Because L&T is the number one company" - it is not a reason, and it is not true in a way
that survives one follow-up.

### Q5. Why not a software or IT role? [H]
**A:** Do not disparage IT. "I could have gone that way, and I know it is a strong market. I did not
choose it because what I enjoy is the physical outcome - I want to see a machine, a plant, a
structure standing and working. Software is not a lesser career; it is just not the one I want." Then
redirect to what you do want.
**Trap:** "Coding is boring" - it invites "then what will you do with Excel every day?"

## 2. The project - the most-repeated question in the entire corpus

### Q6. Walk me through your final-year project. [H]
**A:** This is the highest-value question of the interview. Prepare it as a 2-minute story with a
fixed structure: **problem -> what the machine/system is -> your specific part -> the one number or
decision you owned -> what you learned.** Use a diagram if they let you. Make sure the third beat is
yours alone - "we" for the team, "I" for your piece.
**Push:** "Why this project?" -> Why the topic is worth doing, not why the group chose it.
**Push:** "What was the hardest part?" -> Have one specific answer, not "team coordination".
**Trap:** narrating a report instead of a project. If your answer sounds like your project report
read aloud, it is too long and too passive.

### Q7. What exactly was YOUR contribution? [H]
**A:** The single most probed question in fresher panels, because it separates people who worked from
people who attended. Give one concrete, bounded thing: one calculation, one model, one analysis, one
section of the design, one test you set up. Then say what you did NOT do.
**Push:** "Did anyone check your work?" -> "Yes, X did, and we disagreed about Y, so I re-ran it" is a
strong answer. "No, I did it alone" is also honest and fine.
**Trap:** "We did everything together" - it reads as non-contribution.

### Q8. If you could redesign one part of the project, what would you change and why? [H]
**A:** This is not a trick and it is not a trap - it is a maturity probe. Pick a real limitation you
know about, say the constraint that caused it, and say what you would change. "I would change the
material spec because we had a weight constraint and I would have gone for a higher strength grade
and accepted the cost." Or: "I would have validated it with a test at half scale, which we could not
do, so we relied on simulation."
**Trap:** "Nothing, I would keep it as it is" - reads as no critical thinking.

### Q9. How did you validate your result? [H]
**A:** Say which of these you actually did: hand calculation check, comparison with a standard or a
textbook, simulation convergence check, comparison with a previous project, physical test, or
comparison with published data. If you did not validate, say that plainly and say how you would have.
**Trap:** claiming validation you did not do. This is exactly the kind of thing that collapses when
the panel asks a follow-up on the method.

### Q10. What did you learn that the classroom did not teach you? [H]
**A:** The best answers here are about the gap between theory and reality: a tolerance is a number in
a textbook and minutes of machine time on the floor; a formula assumes ideal conditions and the
shop floor does not; a "simple" assembly has 40 tolerances stacking up. Pick one and make it
concrete. This question is often the hinge of the whole interview for someone with a
training-based internship.

## 3. The internship - the observation-only trap

**The rule for this whole section: you were on a guided plant training visit. You were given a
technical run-down. You took notes. You had no design ownership. Never claim ownership, and never
apologise for it either. The panel is not grading your internship grade - they are grading whether
you can tell the truth and whether you can extract a lesson from what you saw.**

### Q11. What did you do in your internship? [H]
**A:** State the frame in one sentence: "It was a plant training attachment at BHEL Hyderabad, in the
Turbines, Compressors and Gas Turbine division. I was given a technical run-down by the division and
taken through the shops, and I took notes on everything I saw. I was not given design ownership, and
I did not do any design work." Then move immediately into your three observations. The 90-second
version is memorised verbatim in `INTERVIEW_PREP_LT.md` section 3.1 - use that.
**Push:** "So you did not do anything?" -> "That is right - it was a training visit, not a project
assignment. What I did do was study. I wrote down the machine specifications, the tooling logic and
the rotor machining sequence, and I have since read up on why those choices are made."
**Trap:** "It was a great learning experience." (No content. This is the answer interviewers name as
the red flag.) Also: "I was part of the team that worked on..."

### Q12. What is one thing you observed there that surprised you? [H]
**A:** Have this ready separately from the script - they will ask it standalone. Your best observation
is the rotor: "A steam turbine rotor is not designed, it is machined. A large alloy-steel forging goes
through a fixed sequence of nineteen operations, blades are fitted into grooves, shrouding is welded
on, and the rotor is balanced stage-wise before the casing closes over it. The biggest machine in that
plant exists purely for first-job rotor balancing, because imbalance force goes as the square of
speed - so at operating speed, a small imbalance becomes a very large force."
**Push:** "What does that tell you about engineering?" -> "That the drawing is only a third of the
job. The process route and the inspection gate at each operation are the other two-thirds."

### Q13. Why did you intern at BHEL, and why L&T now? [H]
**A:** "BHEL was the closest thing to heavy engineering available to me in my own city, and I wanted
to see how a large machine is actually made before I chose a specialisation. What I took from it is
that I want to work in manufacturing and project execution rather than design-only work. L&T is
where that gets used at scale, with a site at the end of it."
**Trap:** making the two sound like a career plan you invented for them. Keep it simple: I wanted to
see heavy manufacturing, I saw it, I want to work in it.

### Q14. What did you contribute during the internship? [H]
**A:** Be honest and reframe: "I did not contribute design work. What I contributed was attention.
I asked questions, I wrote down the machine specifications and the tooling logic so I could explain
them afterwards, and I understood the run-down well enough to explain it to my own group." Then give
one example of something you did that shows you were present and thinking: a question you asked and
the answer, or a process you did not understand until you saw it.
**Trap:** inflating. If they ask a detail you cannot produce, you are finished.

### Q15. What would you do differently if you repeated it? [M]
**A:** "I would go in with a list of what I wanted to understand, instead of waiting to be shown. I
spent time on the run-down and I would have asked more about the inspection side - how a dimension is
actually accepted - rather than only the machining side."

## 4. Site posting, safety and cost - the IC-head round

These come from the final round, sometimes conducted by the business unit's In-Charge head. The
themes are learning attitude, innovation, **safety awareness** and **cost-consciousness**. This round
is where a strong technical candidate is still rejected.

### Q16. Are you willing to work at a project site? [H]
**A:** Affirmative, with awareness. Do NOT say a blind unqualified yes - panels read that as
insincere, and candidates who said it blindly struggled with site posting during onboarding. Answer:
"Yes. I understand the GET role includes a one-year traineeship, and that a large part of it is
project and site work. I have already seen a plant floor - I did a training attachment at BHEL
Hyderabad - so I know the environment is hard work with safety discipline, and I am ready for it. My
home state is X, and I will need to be based away from there, which I am prepared to do."
**Push:** "What if the site is in a remote area with no network?" -> "I have lived in a small town
with poor connectivity, so I am not depending on it."
**Trap:** "Anywhere in India, no problem" with no reason, or an obvious reluctance that you do not
acknowledge.

### Q17. How would you handle a safety-versus-deadline conflict on a project site? [H]
**A:** This is the signature question. The answer must show an order of operations, not a slogan.
1. Safety is non-negotiable - I stop the unsafe activity first, before I discuss the schedule.
2. I name the specific hazard and the specific risk, not a generic "it is unsafe".
3. I escalate to the supervisor and raise it formally; I do not resolve it at my level.
4. I offer the schedule solution: resequence the work, add a shift, or pre-fabricate off the critical
   path, and say which of these I would try.
5. I cite a real control if you have one: permit to work, toolbox talk, fall protection, hot work
   permit, lockout.
6. I close with the cost logic: rework after an incident and the resulting downtime are the largest
   avoidable cost on a site, so protecting the schedule by protecting the work is the faster route.
**Push:** "What if your supervisor says push on?" -> "I would push on with the control in place and
documented, and I would not work outside the permit. If I was told to do so without the control, I
would escalate further rather than comply."
**Trap:** "I would talk to my manager and fix it" - no order of operations, no named hazard.

### Q18. What is a hazard? What is a near miss? Give an example. [H]
**A:** Hazard = a source or situation with the potential to cause harm (an unguarded rotating shaft, a
live conductor, a hot surface, an unsecured scaffold). Near miss = an unplanned event that did not
cause injury this time but had the potential to do so (a dropped tool near someone's foot, a pipe
fletching a worker, a load swinging outside the exclusion zone). The point of tracking near misses is
that they are free lessons - the outcome was luck, not a control.
**Push:** "What did you see at BHEL?" -> A real observation: unguarded machinery, hot work areas,
movement of heavy components, the shop floor discipline. If you do not remember a specific one, say
what you observed generally about the safety discipline in the shop.
**Trap:** confusing hazard and risk. If they push, add: "risk = likelihood x severity, hazard = the
thing that can cause the harm."

### Q19. What do you understand by cost-consciousness? [M]
**A:** "On a site, the cost that gets measured is not the purchase price of the component, it is the
total cost of the item in the system - material plus installation plus access and scaffolding plus
downtime if it fails plus the cost of the rework. So the cheap part that fails early is the expensive
part." Then give one engineering example: an O-ring or a seal, a bearing selection, a fastener
material - something small where the cost of failure exceeds the cost of the part.
**Push:** "Give a site example" -> "Replacing a failed component on a running plant is not just the
part cost, it is the shutdown, the crew, the access equipment and the schedule."

### Q20. Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly. [M]
**A:** One real story, structured: the situation, what you had to learn, what you did, what came out
of it. The internship is a good source: learning a whole division's machinery in a short visit.
**Trap:** describing an exam you studied for. It is technically a valid example but it does not show
learning under time pressure.

### Q21. Show me that you are innovative / tell me about a time you improved something. [M]
**A:** Innovation at a GET level is not invention. It is "I noticed this was done this way, I asked
why, and I found a simpler way" - a spreadsheet that saved a recalculation, a test rig you built to
avoid a wait, a drawing you made clearer, a process you documented so the next person did not repeat
your mistake. Use the project or a lab. Be ready to say what the benefit was and what it cost.

## 5. HR and closing

### Q22. What are your strengths and weaknesses? [H]
**A:** Strength with evidence - pick one and prove it with a number or a moment. Weakness: a real one,
not "I work too hard", and show what you did about it. "I used to take on too much and my deadlines
slipped, so I now write the scope down and agree it before starting."
**Trap:** the humblebrag weakness ("I care too much") and a strength with no proof.

### Q23. Tell me about a time you failed. [M]
**A:** A real failure with your part in it, what the consequence was, what you changed, and evidence
the change worked. A failure you had no part in is not an answer. Do not pick something so serious
that it disqualifies you, and do not pick a fake failure.

### Q24. How do you work in a team? / Give a conflict example. [M]
**A:** Situation, the disagreement, what you did, the outcome. The strong answers involve you
changing your own position on evidence, or finding a way to test both positions. "I was wrong and I
adjusted" is a strong answer.

### Q25. Where do you see yourself in five years? [M]
**A:** Tie it to the GET role: a few years of hands-on site and manufacturing experience, a specific
competence (manufacturing technology, quality, project execution, a design discipline), and eventually
leading a small team or a work package. "I want to be a good engineer" is too thin.

### Q26. Do you have any questions for us? [H]
**A:** Always ask two or three. Good ones for an L&T GET panel: "What does the first year of the
traineeship actually look like - how much is shop floor versus site versus classroom?" / "Which
business unit would a mechanical GET most often go into?" / "What is the one thing that separates the
GETs who progress quickly from the ones who do not?" Ask about the role, not the CTC - the panel
cannot negotiate and it wastes your one chance to show you are interested.

### Q27. Why should we hire you? [H]
**A:** Three things, one line each, tied to the role: fundamentals I can be trusted with, a real
experience of a heavy manufacturing floor even if it was observational, and a genuine willingness to
work at a site. Do not repeat your introduction verbatim.

### Q28. Extempore - 2 minutes on a topic they give you. [M]
**A:** Topics that recur: safety, teamwork, India's energy transition, Make in India, the future of
manufacturing, engineering education quality, AI in manufacturing, quality and zero defects.
Use a fixed frame so 2 minutes never runs empty: **what the issue is -> why it matters to an
engineer -> one concrete example -> what should be done -> one closing line.** Speak slowly, three
short sections, and stop on time. They score structure, clarity and composure, not depth.
**Trap:** starting with a definition and running out of time before saying anything useful.

### Q29. Do you have any medical issues? Are you colour blind? [M]
**A:** Know this before you walk in. The L&T pre-employment medical includes an **eyesight and a
colour-blindness test**, and field roles are safety-sensitive. If you have any history of eye surgery,
weak eyesight, colour blindness, or a known allergy, declare it. Declaring a manageable condition is
far better than being found out at the medical stage.
**Trap:** hiding it and being caught in the medical.

### Q30. What is your CGPA and are there any backlogs? [H]
**A:** Answer plainly. The official eligibility for the L&T GET drive is a **65% aggregate in 10th,
12th and the degree, with no backlogs**. If you are short on the aggregate, say so honestly and talk
about the trend and the subjects that were strong.

## 6. If they ask something you do not know

**A:** "I do not know that, and I would rather say so than guess. Here is how I would find out: I
would look it up in [the standard / the handbook / the design manual], and I would work it out from
[first principles]." Then, if you can, start doing it out loud - the attempt is worth more than the
answer.

**This is the single most important habit in this file.** Panels report candidates who guessed
confidently and got it wrong far more often than candidates who said "I do not know" and then
reasoned. A wrong confident answer is a character problem; an honest gap is a normal fresher answer.

## 7. The 10-second rules

1. Answer the question that was asked. Do not answer the one you prepared.
2. Numbers first, then the mechanism, then why it matters in a plant.
3. Say "I" for your work, "we" for the team's.
4. Never claim ownership of work you did not do.
5. If you are unsure, say so and then reason out loud.
6. One concrete detail beats three generalities, every time.
7. Do not name a code, a standard or a client you cannot explain.
8. Keep the answer under 60 seconds and let them pull the thread.
9. Safety is never a trade-off against schedule - say the order of operations, not the slogan.
10. "I do not know, here is how I would work it out" is a strong answer. Guessing is not.
